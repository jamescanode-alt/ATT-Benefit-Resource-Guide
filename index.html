The AT&T Union Retirement Benefits Guide

What a new Farther advisor needs to read, explain, and advise on the pension and 401(k) for AT&T's bargained (union) employees, from cash balance mechanics to the decisions that matter most for clients.

Section 01

The benefit landscape

A retiring AT&T union employee may draw income from up to three layers. Understanding how they stack helps you frame the whole picture in a first meeting.

🏛️

AT&T Pension

Employer-funded lifetime benefit. Paid as a monthly annuity or, for many bargained groups, a lump sum. The core of this guide.

💼

AT&T 401(k)

Employee + employer money at Fidelity. Investable, portable, inheritable. Grows with the market.

🇺🇸

Social Security

The federal layer. Not an AT&T benefit, but it shapes claiming timing and the income gap you're solving for.

The advisor's lens

The pension and 401(k) play complementary roles. The pension is a guaranteed-floor / longevity asset; the 401(k) is the flexible, growth-and-legacy asset. A common planning move is to use the guaranteed pension income to cover essential expenses, freeing the 401(k) to be invested for growth and used for discretionary spending and inheritance.

Accuracy first

Figures and tables here are drawn from the Summary Plan Descriptions in your project library. Specific factors, bands, and dollar limits change by contract and by year. Always verify a client's actual numbers in NetBenefits before giving advice. This guide builds intuition; it is not a substitute for the controlling plan documents.

Client example

Meet two clients we'll follow through this guide. Rosa Alvarez, a CWA District 9 (Pacific Bell) service representative, was hired in 1990, so she has both a Pre-99 Pension Band benefit and a post-1998 Cash Balance Account, the blended benefit. Marcus Bell, an IBEW-represented technician hired in 2012, started after the Pre-99 cutoff, so he has a Cash Balance Account only. Same employer, same era of union membership, two very different pension shapes. We'll see how that difference drives every decision that follows.

Section 02

Eligibility & vesting

Two gates control whether a client has a benefit at all: are they an Eligible Employee, and are they vested?

Who is eligible

Eligibility flows from the collective bargaining agreement. An employee is generally eligible if their job title and classification are covered by an applicable CWA or IBEW agreement with a participating AT&T company. Participation begins after completing one year of participation service.

Common exclusions

Management employees, leased employees, independent contractors, employees already earning a benefit under another plan program, and nonresident aliens with no U.S. income are not eligible. A short (≤1 year) "acting" promotion into management does not break eligibility.

How vesting works

Vesting is when the benefit becomes the employee's to keep, even if they leave. The threshold depends on which formula governs the benefit:

Benefit typeVesting requirement
Cash Balance formula (current)3 years of vesting service, or reaching Normal Retirement Age while employed
Older / Pension Band formula5 years of vesting service, or reaching Normal Retirement Age while employed

One year of vesting service is earned by completing a 12-month period of service. Normal Retirement Age is the later of age 65 or completing the required years of participation in the plan.

The advisor's lens

A client who is not yet vested and considering leaving AT&T is making a far bigger decision than they may realize, walking away can mean forfeiting the entire pension. If a client is close to a vesting cliff, that timing belongs in the conversation.

Client example

Rosa Alvarez has service dating to 1990, so she cleared the 5-year bar for her older-formula benefit decades ago, if she walked away today, the pension is entirely hers. Marcus Bell, hired in 2012, is on the 3-year cash-balance schedule; he vested in 2015 and is fully vested now. A colleague who had left at 2 years and 10 months, by contrast, would have forfeited the whole cash-balance benefit. The vesting cliff is unforgiving right up until you clear it, which is exactly why it belongs in any "should I leave?" conversation.

Section 03 · The Pension

Two formulas under one roof

This is the single most important concept in the pension. A client's monthly benefit can be built from two different formulas, depending on when they earned their service.

Cash Balance Account

Service earned 1999 and later

A hypothetical account that grows monthly with pay-based credits plus interest. Looks and feels like a savings account, but it's a defined-benefit promise. Often lump-sum eligible.

Pre-99 Pension Band Benefit

Service earned before Jan 1, 1999

A classic "dollars × years of service" monthly annuity tied to a job-title pension band. Carries the powerful Modified Rule of 75 early-retirement features.

The key question

For any client, ask: "When did you start at AT&T?" Long-tenured employees with pre-1999 service have a blended benefit (both formulas) and the Pre-99 portion may unlock service-pension features the Cash Balance portion does not. Newer employees have a Cash Balance benefit only.

Client example

Rosa, hired in 1990, has both portions: a Pre-99 Pension Band benefit for her 1990–1998 service, plus a Cash Balance Account for everything from 1999 on. Her total pension adds the two together. Marcus, hired in 2012, has no pre-1999 service, so his benefit is Portion A alone, the Cash Balance Account. That single difference, when each of them started, decides whether the powerful Modified Rule of 75 features (Section 06) are even in play.

① Basic Benefit Credit: the main engine

Southeast Program: 60 × the Pension Band Amount

The Southeast SPD states the formula directly: the Basic Benefit Credit (formerly called the pension band credit) equals 60 times the Pension Band Amount for the client's Pension Band Number, using the highest band that applied during the calendar year. Credits are added annually on the last day of the calendar year, or the last day of the month of termination or transfer if earlier, and are pro-rated for a partial year, part-time hours, or a leave of absence.

A client whose highest band during the year was Pension Band 111, with a Pension Band Amount of $57.23, receives a Basic Benefit Credit of 60 × $57.23 = $3,433.80 for that year.

Notice what this means for advice: under the Southeast design, a raise or a heavy overtime year does not increase the pension credit. Only a promotion into a higher band does. Band amounts themselves are renegotiated in bargaining and are published in "Attachment 3" of the SPD. A client with pension service before 1999 who was still employed on or after Jan. 1, 1999 gets an opening Cash Balance Account equal to the greater of their existing accruals or $3,000.

Bargained Cash Balance Program #2: Pension Compensation × an Age Credit Factor

For bargained employees hired after Aug 8, 2009, the account is credited on the last day of each month with Pension Compensation × an Age Credit Factor. Pension Compensation is monthly wages including actual base pay, group incentive compensation, individual nondiscretionary incentive (commissions), group incentive adjustments, short-term disability payments while an employee, and Success Sharing Plan payments not contributed to an HRA. For leveraged job titles in the major core contracts, target incentive payments are also included. The factor rises with age, so the same paycheck buys a bigger credit as the employee gets older:

One group is frozen

Employees of AT&T Mobility Services LLC, IBEW Local 1547 (Blue Contract), in all job titles except Cell Site Technician and Cell Site Tech Foreman, had accruals frozen on Jan 1, 2020. They receive no further Basic Benefit Credits and no Supplemental Pay Credits; only Interest Credits continue to be added. For those clients the account is a compounding balance, not an accruing benefit, which removes the "stay for the accrual" argument entirely.

Worked example

Age 40, monthly Pension Compensation $4,000, Age Credit Factor 2.78%. Basic Benefit Credit = $4,000 × 2.78% = $111.20 added that month.

② Interest Credit: steady compounding

Under the Southeast Program, interest is credited annually, on the last day of each calendar year before payments begin (and pro-rated in the year they do), and it continues to accrue even after termination of employment. The rate is the annual rate on 30-year U.S. Treasury securities published as of November of the prior calendar year, and it is applied to the balance as of the first day of the year, before that year's Basic Benefit Credit is added. Interest begins the calendar year after the account is first credited.

Worked example (Southeast, from the SPD)

Opening balance $10,000; Basic Benefit Credit for the year $3,000; applicable 30-year Treasury rate 4.5%. Interest = $10,000 × 4.5% = $450 (not $13,000 × 4.5%, because the credit is added after interest is figured). Year-end balance = $10,000 + $3,000 + $450 = $13,450.

Under Bargained Cash Balance Program #2, interest is credited monthly, at a monthly rate that compounds to a flat 4.5% annual rate, applied to the balance as of the last day of the prior month (that is, before the current month's Basic Benefit Credit is added). Crediting begins the month after the account first receives a Basic Benefit Credit and continues to the last day of the month before payments begin, even after Termination of Employment. Note this is a fixed 4.5%, not a Treasury-linked rate, which makes it the most predictable of the three engines.

Under the Mobility Program, interest is also credited monthly, but at a monthly rate compounding to the 30-year Treasury rate published as of the second month of the prior calendar quarter. The SPD's own example: a $10,000 balance at a 4% Treasury rate earns $10,000 × 0.327% = $32.70 for the month.

Worked example (Bargained Cash Balance #2, from the SPD)

Prior month-end balance $10,000; this month's Basic Benefit Credit $200. Interest = $10,000 × 0.367% = $36.70 (4.5% annual converted to a monthly rate), figured on the $10,000 before the credit is added. New balance = $10,000 + $200 + $36.70 = $10,236.70.

The advisor's lens

The "interest keeps accruing after you leave" point is worth making explicitly to a Southeast client weighing a deferred pension against a lump sum. The account does not freeze at termination, it keeps earning the Treasury-linked rate until payments begin. Whether that beats taking the lump sum and investing it is exactly the comparison to run, and the answer moves with the rate environment rather than being settled once.

③ Supplemental Pay Credit: higher earners only (Bargained Cash Balance #2)

Scope: this credit exists in Bargained Cash Balance Program #2 only. Southeast has no wage-base credit of any kind (its credit is band-driven), and the Mobility SPD states a flat 5% with no supplemental component. Skip this subsection for those clients.

Social Security only taxes and replaces income up to an annual ceiling, the Social Security Wage Base ($184,500 in 2026, indexed up each year, current figure at ssa.gov: contribution & benefit base). The Supplemental Pay Credit adds extra pension credit on the slice of pay above that ceiling, the compensation Social Security won't be replacing in retirement. In any year where year-to-date Pension Compensation crosses the wage base, the excess earns an extra 2% credit, added monthly alongside the Basic Benefit and Interest Credits.

First month over the threshold: 2% × all year-to-date pay above the wage base. Each later month that year: 2% × that month's full Pension Compensation.

Worked example

Year-to-date Pension Compensation $189,500 as of Nov 30; wage base $184,500. Excess = $5,000 → credit = $5,000 × 2% = $100 that month. December's credit would then be that month's pay × 2%.

Most bargained clients on standard base pay never reach the wage base and never see this credit. It tends to surface for clients in leveraged or commission-heavy titles, or in an unusually large overtime or incentive year. Note the rate is a flat 2%, unlike the age-stepped Basic Benefit Credit.

Don't confuse these two

Two similarly-named items belong to different programs entirely and are calculated in completely different ways. They cannot appear on the same statement:

Supplemental Pay Credit — Bargained Cash Balance Program #2 only (bargained hires after Aug 8, 2009). A 2% credit on Pension Compensation above the Social Security Wage Base, added monthly to the cash balance account.

Supplemental Monthly Pension Benefit — the Pre-99 Pension Band formula, so Southeast and the other legacy band programs. Based on in-charge allowances, shift/night differentials, and temporary-promotion pay over the 36 months before retirement (average annual supplemental pay × pre-99 service × 0.001). See Section 05.

The tell is the hire date: a client with pre-99 service cannot be in Bargained Cash Balance #2, and a post-2009 hire has no Pre-99 band benefit. If you think you are looking at both, you are looking at the wrong program.

The advisor's lens

"The account accelerates late in your career" is a common framing, and whether it is true depends entirely on the program, so check before using it. Under Bargained Cash Balance #2 it holds twice over: the Age Credit Factor steps up at 30, 37, 44, and 50, and interest compounds on a larger balance. That makes the years just before 50 genuinely expensive to walk away from, and the step from 3.28% to 4.04% is the single biggest one. Under Southeast it holds only through compounding, because the credit is 60 × the band amount and does not rise with age at all. Under Mobility, likewise, the credit is a flat 5% at every age. Using the age-graded version of this argument on a Southeast or Mobility client overstates the cost of leaving early.

Client example: Marcus Bell

Marcus was hired in 2012, after the Aug 8, 2009 cutoff that closes West, Southeast, Midwest, East, and Legacy Bargained. That places him in Bargained Cash Balance Program #2 (NIN 78-74506), the age-graded design above.

At 40 with monthly Pension Compensation of $4,000, his Age Credit Factor is 2.78%, so this month's Basic Benefit Credit = $4,000 × 2.78% = $111.20. His account entered the month at $10,000, which earns 0.367% (the monthly equivalent of the flat 4.5%) ≈ $36.70, figured before the credit is added. Month-end balance = $10,000 + $111.20 + $36.70 = $10,147.90. He is under the Social Security Wage Base, so no Supplemental Pay Credit applies.

The retire-or-stay lesson is real for him: at 44 his factor steps to 3.28% and at 50 to 4.04%, so that same $4,000 paycheck will buy $161.60 instead of $111.20. His account accelerates in exactly the later years an early exit would give up. One thing to confirm rather than assume: because he is IBEW, check whether his early service falls under the alternate 1.75%–4.00% ladder, which ran until Jan 1, 2014 for the IBEW contracts. If it does, his first two years accrued at the lower rate.

The advisor's lens

The Pre-99 portion is the part of the benefit that carries the Modified Rule of 75 and its early-retirement subsidies (next section). It is also where a client may qualify for retiree health and welfare eligibility. For long-tenured clients this small-looking annuity can be the deciding factor in when to retire.

Client example: Rosa Alvarez

Rosa's job title mapped to Pension Band 111, whose Pension Band Amount is $57.23 under the current SPD's tables, and she has 9 years of Pension Calculation Service as of 12/31/1998 (1990–1998). She has 36 years of service, so she satisfies Mod 75, and at age 56 that puts her Age Incentive Factor at 1.00, not 1.06. Her Basic Monthly Pension Benefit = $57.23 × 9 × 1.00 = $515.07/month. Add a small Supplemental Monthly Pension Benefit for the shift differentials she earned in her final three pre-retirement years, and this Pre-99 annuity, modest on its own, is the piece that carries her Modified Rule of 75 rights and retiree-healthcare eligibility (Section 06). Marcus, hired in 2012, has none of this.

Worth showing her the sensitivity: if she waits to commence at 62, the same 9 years and band produce $57.23 × 9 × 1.06 = $545.97, about 6% more on this slice, entirely from the Age Incentive Factor. Small in isolation, but it runs in the same direction as everything else about waiting, and it is a number she has almost certainly never been shown. Note also that band amounts are renegotiated in bargaining and the current SPD updated them, so re-derive from "Attachment 3" rather than reusing a figure from an older edition.

Section 06

The Modified Rule of 75

"Mod 75" determines whether a client can retire early without the steep reduction that normally applies, and it's where advisors most often catch costly client misconceptions.

A client satisfies Mod 75 when their age and years of service hit one of these combinations:

AgeYears of service required
Any age30
5025
5520
65 or older10

Eligibility is tested at Termination of Employment, using completed whole years of age and service. The status is locked in the moment the client separates, it cannot be earned afterward.

Separating before vs. after satisfying Mod 75

This is the line that changes a client's retirement. The same person can walk away with a dramatically different benefit depending on which side of Mod 75 they're standing on when they leave.

If they separate BEFORE meeting Mod 75

They leave with only a Deferred Vested Pension (assuming they're vested at all). Three consequences follow:

Locked out permanently. Because eligibility is frozen at termination, they can never "age into" a service pension later, even after they're old enough on paper. The door closes when they walk out.

Heavy early-commencement reductions. When they later start the Pre-99 benefit, it's cut by the program's early-reduction factors based on their age at commencement, a much steeper haircut than the Mod 75 subsidy. They also generally can't commence until age 55 (with 20 yrs) or age 50 (with 25 yrs).

No retiree healthcare. A former bargained employee eligible only for a Deferred Vested Pension is not an eligible former employee for AT&T retiree medical. This is often the largest loss of all.

What they GAIN once they meet Mod 75

Crossing the threshold converts a deferred vested benefit into a service pension, which unlocks:

Subsidized (or zero) early-retirement reduction. With 30+ years, the Pre-99 benefit is fully unreduced at any age. With fewer than 30 years, it's reduced only 0.5%/month before the threshold age (55 or 56 by contract), far gentler than the deferred-vested factors, and zero once they reach that age.

Access to retiree health & welfare. Satisfying Mod 75 (or qualifying for a Disability Pension) at termination is what makes a client a candidate for subsidized AT&T retiree medical, the bridge that matters most for pre-65 retirees.

The ability to start sooner, with more. Service-pension status removes the "wait until 50/55 to commence" gate that constrains a deferred vested benefit.

The leaving-too-early scenario

A client a few months short of a Mod 75 milestone who resigns is often trading away both a large unreduced-pension subsidy and retiree-medical eligibility, frequently a six-figure decision. The cash balance portion is unaffected (it's just the account value), but the Pre-99 service-pension features and healthcare access can be lost for good. Always map exact age and service before a client gives notice.

Four traps to flag

1. It's specific pairs, not simple addition. "My age plus my service is 75, so I qualify" is the classic mistake, the combinations are fixed thresholds (multiples of 5), not any sum reaching 75.

2. You can't "age into" service-pension eligibility after you leave. Eligibility is tested at termination. A vested-but-not-service-pension-eligible client who quits early is locked out of the subsidy.

3. A vested pension that misses Mod 75 is severely penalized for early commencement. The reductions are large.

4. Meeting Mod 75 doesn't always mean "no reduction." Only 30+ years of service guarantees a fully unreduced early pension. With fewer than 30 years, the benefit is still cut 0.5% per month (6% per year) for every month commenced before the contract's threshold age, age 55 (Craft/West) or age 56 (Southeast).

For example, satisfying Mod 75 with 25 years of service and commencing at age 53 under the West rules is 24 months early × 0.5% = a 12% permanent reduction. The takeaway: "I made Rule of 75" is not the same as an unreduced benefit, always check service years and the client's contract threshold age.

The advisor's lens

When a client is close to a Mod 75 milestone, a few extra months of work can be worth tens of thousands in lifetime income, turning a penalized vested pension into an unreduced service pension, and sometimes unlocking retiree healthcare. Mapping the client's exact age/service against this table is one of the highest-leverage things you do.

Retiree healthcare access: often the bigger prize (but read the fine print)

Satisfying the Modified Rule of 75 (or qualifying for a Disability Pension) at termination is the gate to eligibility to enroll in AT&T retiree medical/dental, governed by the AT&T Medical Program SPD. That access, the ability to buy into a group plan before Medicare, is genuinely valuable. But "eligible to enroll" is not the same as "subsidized," and for most of today's workforce the subsidy is gone.

The subsidy has been largely eliminated

Two separate questions: can you enroll (Mod 75) and will the Company pay part of the premium (subsidy). On the subsidy:

Hired on/after 1/1/2001 → you pay 100% of the cost (no subsidy), always. Only pre-2001 hires may qualify, under legacy-company rules (Legacy S/T/C).

The 65+ Medicare subsidy was eliminated for both bargained and management retirees whose last day on payroll is on/after 1/1/2021.

For union/bargained retirees who do qualify, pre-Medicare (under-65) subsidized contributions still exist under the Bargained Contribution Rules (tiered Bronze/Silver/Gold by legacy group), the management-side pre-Medicare cut (last day on/after 1/1/2022) did not apply to bargained employees. Confirm the client's specific eligibility in their Total Reward statement.

Why access still matters most for pre-65 retirees

A client who retires before age 65 is not yet Medicare-eligible. Individual coverage in that gap can run well over $1,000–$2,000/month per person. Even unsubsidized access to the AT&T group retiree plan (enrolled through Alight) can beat the open market, and where a bargained subsidy still applies it is worth materially more. A client leaving a few months short of Mod 75 can lose the eligibility to enroll at all, still often a six-figure decision.

Verify: don't assume

Eligibility, subsidy status, contributions, and plan designs are governed by the separate Medical Program SPD and the current bargaining agreement, not the pension SPD, and have changed materially (see the Dec 2020 retiree-benefit changes). Confirm the client's specific situation before promising anything.

Client example: Rosa Alvarez

Rosa is 56 with 36 years of service. Because she has 30-plus years, she satisfies Mod 75 outright and her Pre-99 benefit is unreduced at any age, she could have commenced it at 52 with no early-commencement cut.

Contrast a counterfactual: had Rosa instead left at 53 with 25 years (the 50/25 combination, but under 30 years), commencing at 53 under the West rules would be 24 months before the age-55 threshold × 0.5%/month = a 12% permanent reduction on the Pre-99 piece. And a colleague who resigned at 49 with 19 years would miss Mod 75 entirely, forfeiting both the subsidy and retiree-medical eligibility. Rosa's cash-balance portion is unaffected in every case, it's just the account value.

Two features worth naming to the client

The pop-up. Every Southeast J&S option carries a pop-up: if the survivor dies first, the retiree's benefit reverts to the unreduced Single Life amount. That materially changes the J&S calculus, because the usual objection ("I take a permanent haircut and might outlive them anyway") is answered by the plan itself. The $75,000 Partial Lump Sum. A Mod 75 satisfier can take exactly $75,000 in cash and keep the rest as a residual annuity. This is the middle path clients most often want and least often know exists: enough liquidity to clear a mortgage, fund a bridge to Social Security, or seed an emergency reserve, without surrendering the longevity hedge. It is not available to Special Represented Employees, and it is not a variable amount. Raise it explicitly during the annuity-versus-lump-sum conversation rather than framing that decision as binary.

Note that a Pension Band benefit for a client who does not satisfy Mod 75 has no lump-sum option at all, only annuities. For a client whose retirement math assumed a lump sum, whether they clear Mod 75 is therefore not just a question of how much, it can be a question of whether the option exists.

Small-benefit cash-out

If the present value of the pension is ≤ $7,000, the plan will automatically cash out or roll over the benefit (≤ $1,000 as a single lump sum; over $1,000 to $7,000 as an automatic IRA rollover unless the client elects otherwise). This threshold rose from $5,000 to $7,000 effective Jan 1, 2024 (2024 SMM, applies across all programs). The normal election rules don't apply. Flag this so a client isn't surprised by an automatic distribution.

The advisor's lens: annuity vs. lump sum

This is the marquee pension decision. The annuity is a longevity hedge with a guaranteed floor and no market risk; the lump sum offers control, growth potential, flexibility, and a legacy asset but transfers investment and longevity risk to the client. The two factors to weigh most carefully are the client's current health and family medical history: the annuity grows more attractive the longer the client is likely to outlive the IRS mortality tables. Also weigh other guaranteed income, spousal needs, legacy goals, and the annuity's implied "payout rate" versus what a diversified portfolio could reasonably support. There is no universal right answer, but there's a right answer for this client.

Tax mechanic to know

A lump sum paid directly to the client triggers a mandatory 20% federal withholding. A direct rollover to an IRA or other eligible plan avoids withholding and preserves tax deferral, usually the cleaner path when a lump sum is chosen.

Timing the lump sum around interest rates

Whenever a monthly pension (annuity) is converted to a lump sum, the amount is a present value calculated with the IRS minimum present-value segment rates, so it moves inversely to interest rates: when rates rise, the lump sum falls; when rates fall, it grows. AT&T locks the prior-November rates for the entire commencement calendar year, so the year a client commences can swing the lump sum materially, in a rapidly rising-rate year, commencing this year vs. next can be a double-digit-percent difference. (This is the defined-benefit annuity conversion; a pure cash-balance account is simply its balance and is not rate-converted.) Model the client's likely commencement-year rate rather than guessing, see irs.gov: minimum present-value segment rates.

Client example: Rosa Alvarez

Rosa's NetBenefits statement shows a combined Single Life Annuity of $2,300/month (her Pre-99 band benefit plus the annuitized Cash Balance Account). She's married, so her default is the 50% Joint & Survivor: $2,300 × 90% = $2,070/month to Rosa, with $1,035/month continuing to her husband if she dies first. Section 09

Crossing between union and management

A client who moves from a bargained job into management, or gets demoted or transfers back, does not carry one benefit across the line. They accumulate separate benefits on each side, governed by different rules, and some things bridge while others stop dead. This section is the map of what travels and what does not.

Start here: a transfer is not a termination

The single fact that organizes everything below. Termination of Employment is defined as the date you terminate employment with all members of the AT&T Controlled Group. Moving from a CWA job into management keeps the client inside that group, so it is not a Termination of Employment and not a break in service. Their Term of Employment (also called seniority or net credited service) keeps running without interruption.

That matters more than almost anything else in this guide, because Term of Employment is what drives the Modified Rule of 75 and, through it, retiree healthcare eligibility. A client who promotes into management keeps building toward Mod 75. Clients frequently assume the opposite and turn down promotions over it.

The pension: two benefits, not one

Every program SPD says a version of the same thing: on a move to another program, "you will be eligible to receive a distribution of your available vested Pension Benefit from both this Program and any other applicable Plan program." The pieces do not merge and they do not compete. They stack.

For a client leaving a cash-balance program (Bargained Cash Balance #2, Mobility, or the cash-balance half of Southeast), the SPD is explicit: "your Cash Balance Account will remain in this Program and will continue to earn Interest Credits." So it keeps compounding at that program's rate but stops receiving Basic Benefit Credits and Supplemental Pay Credits. It becomes a frozen, growing balance.

Union → management: the three-year service bridge, and its big exception

Moving into the Nonbargained Program triggers a service-bridging rule that is genuinely valuable, and a carve-out that quietly cancels it for a large group:

The Nonbargained rule, verbatim in substance

The benefit is the sum of two pieces. Once the client earns three years of service in the Nonbargained Program, their prior bargained service is included in Pension Calculation Service, which feeds the CAM and PBM formulas. But the resulting benefit is then reduced by the amount of the benefit payable from the prior program, so this is an offset, not double-counting.

The exception: prior service does not bridge if it was "earned under a cash balance formula."

Who that exception hits, and it is most people

A bargained employee hired after Aug 8, 2009 is in Bargained Cash Balance Program #2, which is a cash-balance formula. So their union service does not bridge at all into the management benefit formula. Their BCB#2 account simply sits and earns its 4.5% interest while they accrue separately on the management side.

The bridge is therefore mostly a legacy benefit: it helps a long-tenured client whose union service was earned under a Pension Band formula (West, Midwest, Southeast's pre-99 piece, Legacy Bargained, Mobility Bargained). Establish which formula produced the client's union service before promising anything.

The advisor's lens

Put those two tables together and a specific, common client emerges: a union employee hired after 2009 who is offered a management promotion today. Management → union: demotion, and the frozen band

The reverse direction has its own mechanics. Southeast, for example, warns that "your Pension Calculation Service may not include service earned under certain management program(s) from which you were demoted", so management service does not automatically flow back into the union formula either.

Where the client lands in a Pension Band program, the band itself is protected on the way down:

The demotion freeze

On a Demotion (a move to a job title with a lower Pension Band Amount), the higher band amount is frozen as of the demotion date and continues to be used for the Basic Monthly Pension Benefit until the new title's band amount catches up to it. The client is not immediately dropped to the lower band.

A stronger rule for involuntary cases: if the demotion follows a permanent medical restriction or certain force surpluses, and the client had already satisfied Mod 75, the higher band including any contractual increases is used up to the fifth anniversary of the demotion. That is a materially better outcome, and it turns on why the demotion happened. Establish the reason and get it documented.

One transfer is genuinely absorbing rather than additive: a move from the Southeast Management Program into the Southeast Program. There, the management cash balance account becomes the opening Cash Balance Account in Southeast and no separate benefit is paid from the management program. It is the exception that proves the rule; every other transfer leaves two benefits.

Promotions, demotions, and the one-year test

Short-term moves are handled deliberately so that a temporary assignment does not reshuffle a career's worth of benefit:

Acting or temporary promotion, one year or less

The employee stays in the union savings plan. Both the ARSP and the BSSP name this case explicitly: someone covered by a bargaining agreement who is "temporarily promoted to a management position for one year or less" remains an Eligible Employee of that plan. On the pension side, a temporary promotion does not take effect for band purposes until one year in the new position, and if it lasts less than a year the wage difference is instead picked up as a Supplemental Payment feeding the Supplemental Monthly Pension Benefit.

Permanent promotion, and the 18-month band rule

A permanent Promotion into a higher band only reaches the pension calculation if the client retires 18 months or more after it. Retire within 18 months of a promotion and the Basic Monthly Pension Benefit is computed on the old, lower band. For a client weighing a late-career promotion against a near-term retirement date, those 18 months are a real number worth putting on the calendar.

The 401(k): the account does not follow the client

Two accounts, not one

Both savings-plan SPDs are unambiguous and say the same thing from both directions: "If you become eligible to participate in another plan sponsored by AT&T, your account will remain in the Plan, but no further Contributions or Company Match will be made to your Plan account," and "You do not have the option to transfer the account balance in the other plan to the Plan."

So a legacy Southeast client who promotes into management ends up with a frozen BSSP account and a new ARSP account. Both are at Fidelity, but they are separate plans with separate investment elections and separate beneficiary designations. The stranded account still allows loans and withdrawals; it just stops receiving money.

The upside: the match tier can jump

Management employees are eligible for the Company Match upon hire, with no one-year wait. And the ARSP's enhanced tier applies to AT&T Management Employees "hired, rehired, or transferred on or after Jan 1, 2015", and that word transferred is doing real work. A union employee promoting into management today generally lands on 133⅓% of the first 3% plus 100% of the next 3%, a maximum of 7% of pay.

For a client coming off the BellSouth Utility Operations tier (25% of the first 6%, or 1.5% of pay) or a BSSP band-capped match of roughly $2,474 a year, that is a large and immediate raise in company money. It frequently outweighs the pension accrual they are giving up.

Management → union: the plans are not symmetric

Going the other direction, the two union plans treat a former manager very differently. Under the ARSP, someone in a management position who transfers into a covered bargaining unit is eligible to participate while represented by it. Under the BSSP, a client who was in a management position on Aug 8, 2009 and later transfers into one of its bargaining units is not eligible for the plan at all.

Practical effect: a demotion or voluntary step back into a legacy Southeast union role does not put the client into the BSSP. Confirm which plan they actually land in rather than assuming the union job carries the union plan.

Loans aggregate across both plans

A detail that surprises clients holding two accounts. Loan limits are applied across all plans in the AT&T Controlled Group: the recordkeeper combines vested balances and outstanding loan balances, and the highest-balance-in-the-prior-12-months test is computed on the combined figure. The ARSP states it plainly: two outstanding loans in a frozen account leaves room for only one in the active plan. Check the stranded account before telling a client how much they can borrow.

The advisor's lens: five questions at any crossing

Client example: Marcus Bell

Marcus, the IBEW technician hired in 2012, is offered a first-level management job. Here is his actual picture. His Bargained Cash Balance #2 account stops accruing and keeps earning 4.5% interest. Against that: his Term of Employment keeps running, so he continues building toward Mod 75 and the retiree-medical eligibility that rides on it; his 401(k) match moves to the 133⅓%/100% tier worth up to 7% of pay, immediately and with no service wait; and his ARSP account is unaffected because he was already in the ARSP, not the BSSP, so there is no stranded second account. Run the frozen-pension-accrual number against the incremental match and the raise, and give him a figure rather than a shrug. That conversation is the whole value of knowing this section.

What does not carry over to the new account

Must be re-established on BrokerageLink Roth

These features do not transfer automatically, even when they already exist on the current BrokerageLink account:

Third-party authorization. Any third-party access, including advisor access, requires new paperwork on the BrokerageLink Roth account. For any client whose BrokerageLink account we're authorized on today, this is the item most likely to interrupt service, so get the paperwork moving ahead of the transfer.

Automatic investment of payroll contributions. If BrokerageLink contributions are automatically invested into preselected mutual funds, that instruction has to be set up separately on the Roth account.

Dividend reinvestment elections. Mutual fund dividends reinvest in the same fund either way, but dividends from other eligible securities default to the core position, Fidelity Government Cash Reserves, unless the client enrolls in the dividend reinvestment program on the new account.

Cost basis. Cost basis does not carry over on transferred securities, so gain and loss since the original purchase can no longer be tracked in the new account. This does not change how a distribution from the plan is taxed.

How Fidelity splits an existing account

The Roth balance is calculated by combining the cost basis of all Roth contributions and their associated earnings, with share prices set two business days before the transfer. That balance is divided by the balance of eligible divisible securities to produce a Roth percentage, which is then applied to every divisible holding to decide how many shares move across. Rights and warrants, unpriced or worthless securities, restricted securities, and fractional shares outside the dividend reinvestment program are excluded from the calculation. Shares round to three decimals (two for core cash), while bonds, bills, notes, and CDs round down to the nearest 1,000-unit increment and drop out of the calculation entirely below 1,000 units. Any overage or shortage of $100 or less is trued up in core cash; more than $100 triggers a second proration on what's left. An account that is 100% Roth simply transfers in kind.

Expect cosmetic side effects: a round lot can become an odd lot, trailing dividends on a transferred position post to and stay in the existing account, and a resulting position of less than one whole share is liquidated unless the client has opted into fractional share trading.

The hard deadline: unsplittable holdings get sold

If a preliminary review finds a holding that has to be liquidated for the Roth and non-Roth balances to separate, Fidelity will attempt to contact the client. If the client has not acted by September 14, 2026, AT&T has directed Fidelity to sell BrokerageLink assets on their behalf. The selling order is mutual funds first (highest balance, and on a tie the higher share price), then equities on the same rule, then fixed income (lowest market value first, bonds before CDs). In most cases the amount is $3.00 or less, and above that the sale is capped at the amount needed plus a 10% buffer for market movement. Trade expenses and any realized gain or loss are borne by the existing BrokerageLink account, though commissions on these trades are waived while that stays administratively feasible. Concentrated or illiquid positions are where this bites, so review BrokerageLink holdings with the client well before Sept 14.

Future Roth contributions

Two more limits that bite before 415(c) does

The $360,000 compensation cap

The plan counts only the first $360,000 of Compensation in 2026. The consequence is operational, not just theoretical: the SPD states that on reaching the cap, the client's contributions are automatically suspended for the rest of the year. For a very high earner contributing a level percentage, that means deferrals, and therefore the per-pay-period match, can stop before December. This is the same trap as front-loading, arriving by a different route, and it is a reason to check the spillover election and the contribution rate together for anyone approaching $360,000.

Highly compensated employee testing

A client is a highly compensated employee for the 2026 plan year if their 2025 Compensation exceeded $160,000. HCEs can have their before-tax, after-tax, or match contributions reduced, recharacterized, or partially returned as a result of nondiscrimination testing. Practically, this means an aggressive after-tax strategy for an HCE can be trimmed after the fact, so set expectations that the mega-backdoor number is a target, not a guarantee, and watch for a corrective distribution early in the following year.

Catch-up contributions: Roth is now mandatory for higher earners

Clients age 50+ can make additional elective deferrals above the standard limit, with an enhanced "super catch-up" for those ages 60–63. The SECURE 2.0 Roth-catch-up rule is in force in the ARSP now: a participant who is age 50 or older by the end of the year and earned more than $150,000 in FICA wages in 2025 must make catch-up contributions on a Roth basis only. This is stated flatly in the July 2026 SPD, with no bargained-plan deferral attached, so treat it as live for AT&T's union population, not pending.

Two mechanics that trip clients up. Catch-up requires a separate election in 1% increments ("Employee Pre-tax Catch-Up" and/or "Employee Roth Catch-Up"), and the plan will not accept it unless the client's Basic Contribution election is above 0%. And catch-up is excluded from spillover: it simply stops when the annual catch-up limit is reached rather than continuing as after-tax. Clients ages 60–63 are defaulted toward the higher $11,250 limit and must log in to NetBenefits to reduce it if they don't want it.

The BSSP trap: high earners lose catch-up entirely

The same federal rule lands very differently in the BellSouth Savings and Security Plan. Because the BSSP does not permit Roth contributions at all, and the law requires these clients' catch-up to be Roth, the BSSP SPD states the consequence directly: for a BSSP participant age 50+ with more than $150,000 of 2025 FICA wages, the catch-up contribution limit is $0. Not reduced, not redirected, unavailable. A senior legacy-Southeast technician with heavy overtime can cross $150,000 and lose access to catch-up without ever being told why. Flag it before the client budgets around a contribution they cannot make.

The advisor's lens

For most union clients the 415(c) limit is invisible, match plus modest deferrals never approach it. But for a high-earning, high-saving client, the gap between their deferrals + match and the $72,000 total is a large, underused Roth opportunity. The sequence to coach: capture the full match every pay period → max the 402(g) deferral (pre-tax or Roth per career stage) → fill remaining 415(c) room with after-tax contributions → convert those to Roth promptly.

The four funding buckets

A 401(k) account is built from four distinct funding sources. Understanding how employee choices interact with employer money is essential for maximizing the plan.

①

Pre-tax

Deducted before tax; grows tax-deferred; taxed as ordinary income at withdrawal. Counts toward the elective-deferral limit.

②

Roth

Contributed after tax; qualified growth and withdrawals are tax-free. Also counts toward the elective-deferral limit.

③

After-tax (non-Roth)

A separate, often-overlooked bucket above the deferral limit. Contributed after tax; only the growth is taxable, which is exactly why it pairs with conversion.

④

Employer Match

Company-funded matching dollars matching a percentage of pay up to a bargained cap. Helps build baseline wealth automatically.

Pre-tax vs. Roth: a career-stage decision

Whether a client should direct 401(k) contributions to pre-tax or Roth turns largely on their tax rate now versus their expected tax rate when the money comes out. Career stage is usually the best proxy.

Early career / lower-earning years → lean Roth

A younger or lower-paid union employee is often in a low marginal bracket, so the deduction from a pre-tax contribution is worth little. Paying tax now at a low rate and letting decades of growth come out tax-free is frequently the stronger trade. Roth dollars also don't add to MAGI in retirement, which helps with future IRMAA and Social Security taxation.

Peak-earning years → lean pre-tax

A senior employee at the top of the wage scale (plus heavy overtime) may be in a high bracket where the pre-tax deduction is genuinely valuable. Deferring now and drawing later (potentially in lower-income early-retirement years before pension/Social Security/RMDs stack up) can lower the lifetime tax bill.

It's rarely all-or-nothing. Tax diversification (holding both pre-tax and Roth buckets) gives the client levers to manage MAGI year by year in retirement (e.g., drawing from Roth in a year they'd otherwise cross an IRMAA tier).

After-tax contributions → the "mega backdoor Roth"

The after-tax bucket is powerful because of what you do next: if the plan allows, the client can immediately convert after-tax dollars to Roth (in-plan, or via in-service rollover to a Roth IRA). Converting right away means little or no growth has accrued yet, so there's little or no tax on the conversion, and from that point the money grows tax-free. The longer after-tax dollars sit unconverted, the more taxable growth builds up, so the move is to convert promptly and regularly.

Prerequisites to confirm

This only works if the AT&T plan permits both (a) after-tax contributions and (b) in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals. Confirm both in NetBenefits before building a strategy around it.

Getting money out: loans, withdrawals, and the cash-out floor

Loans

ARSP: up to three outstanding loans, of which one may be a principal-residence loan (1–10 years); general-purpose loans run 1–5 years. Minimum $1,000; maximum is the lesser of $50,000 less the highest balance in the prior 12 months, or 50% of the vested balance. Interest is prime + 1%. BSSP allows only two general-purpose loans and no residence loan. Note the count is across all AT&T plans, so a frozen account holding two loans leaves room for only one more.

DFA and BrokerageLink balances are excluded from the loan calculation and cannot be borrowed against. Default happens if a missed payment is not cured by the end of the following calendar quarter, and the balance is then reported as taxable income.

Withdrawals while employed

ARSP types: General, Before-tax Hardship, SOP, Roth, Rollover, Military, and Domestic Abuse. Before-tax money is reachable through a General Withdrawal only if the client is age 59½ or Disabled; after-tax money and vested match are available at any age. The Domestic Abuse Withdrawal is newly documented: up to the lesser of $10,500 or 50% of the vested account, within one year of the incident, and repayable within three years. BSSP offers a narrower set (no SOP, no Roth) and limits General Withdrawals to one per 6-month period.

The mandatory cash-out floor rose to $7,000

Both savings plans raised the threshold from $1,000 to $7,000. Accounts of $1,000 or less are still paid out as a lump sum on separation. Accounts above $1,000 but not more than $7,000 are now automatically direct-rolled to an IRA unless the client elects otherwise. Above $7,000, nothing happens without an election. The pension plan moved on the same axis, from $5,000 to $7,000.

The practical risk is a small balance from a short stint being swept into a default IRA the client never monitors. Catch it at separation, not years later.

One security detail worth knowing before a client updates their profile: regardless of account size, a distribution cannot be paid for at least 15 days after an address change. If a client is moving and retiring in the same window, sequence the address change well ahead of the distribution request.

At separation: two traps to handle

The Rule of 55, and how a rollover forfeits it

If a client separates from AT&T in the year they turn 55 or older, they can take withdrawals from the AT&T 401(k) penalty-free (no 10% early-withdrawal penalty) while the money stays in the plan, a valuable bridge for an early retiree before age 59½. Rolling the 401(k) to an IRA forfeits this: an IRA has no Rule of 55, so the client is back to the 59½ threshold. Before recommending a full rollover for a 55–59 client, check whether they'll need penalty-free access first, it can be worth leaving some or all in the plan. Note the plan may also limit how those withdrawals can be taken (e.g., not flexible monthly payments), confirm with Fidelity.

Handle an outstanding 401(k) loan before rolling over

If the client has a 401(k) loan when they leave, address it deliberately, three paths: (1) Pay it off before the rollover (allow ~72 hours for the $0 balance to post, or the rollover can inadvertently trigger a default); (2) Let it default, the outstanding balance is issued on a 1099-R as a taxable distribution (plus the 10% penalty if under 59½ with no exemption); or (3) Keep it by leaving enough balance in the plan and continuing payments. Flag this early, an overlooked loan is a common, avoidable tax surprise at rollover.

Watch the per-pay-period match trap

Front-loading can forfeit match

AT&T's employer match is calculated each pay period, not annually, and the SPD describes no "true-up" that retroactively makes up missed match. Any pay period where the client contributes less than the matched cap (generally 6% of that paycheck's pay) leaves match behind for good. The plan's default spillover election softens the classic front-loading trap: once a client hits the annual deferral limit, contributions automatically continue as after-tax and keep earning the per-paycheck match. But if the client has opted out of spillover, or suspends contributions after front-loading, the match stops with them, leaving employer money on the table. Confirm the spillover election in NetBenefits before a client front-loads.

Also note: the match is not limited to pre-tax and Roth deferrals. The SPD defines the matched Basic Contributions as pre-tax, Roth, after-tax, or any combination, so after-tax (bucket ③) dollars within the matched cap earn the same match, and that's exactly why spillover keeps the match alive after the deferral limit. The only employee contributions that never earn match are catch-up contributions (age 50+) and anything above the matched cap (the first 6% of Compensation).

Coordinating pension & 401(k) income with IRMAA in mind

Once a client reaches Medicare, their premiums are no longer flat. The Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA) adds a surcharge to Medicare Part B and Part D premiums when modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) crosses a threshold. Pension income, 401(k) withdrawals, and Roth conversions all feed MAGI, so how a client sequences distributions directly drives what they pay for Medicare.

Cliff, not a rampCrossing a threshold by even $1 triggers the full surcharge for that tier, there's no phase-in. The dollar that puts a client over can carry a very high effective marginal cost.
Per personFor a married couple, both Medicare-enrolled spouses pay the surcharge once joint MAGI crosses a tier.

For reference, 2026 IRMAA begins above $109,000 MAGI (single) and $218,000 (married filing jointly), on a five-tier sliding scale (topping out at $500,000 single / $750,000 MFJ). Because the thresholds and surcharge amounts change yearly, always pull the current figures rather than relying on a remembered number: medicare.gov: Medicare costs & IRMAA.

The advisor's lens

A retiree in, say, the 22% federal bracket who nudges over an IRMAA line can face a combined effective marginal rate well above the headline bracket once the surcharge is counted. The practical move is to project MAGI for each year and size distributions and conversions to stay just under the next tier, especially in the years before age 65 and in the lookback years feeding the first Medicare premiums. Note: a voluntary Roth conversion or asset sale is not a qualifying event for an IRMAA appeal, so it has to be planned, not appealed.

Roth conversions with brackets and IRMAA in view

Converting pre-tax dollars to Roth means voluntarily recognizing income now to buy tax-free growth and future MAGI control. The window that often makes this attractive for AT&T clients is the gap between retiring and the onset of Social Security and required minimum distributions, a stretch of unusually low taxable income, especially for someone who retired early under Mod 75.

How to size a conversion

The disciplined approach is to "fill the bracket": convert just enough to use up a low or moderate tax bracket without spilling into the next one, and, once the client is within two years of Medicare or already enrolled, without crossing the next IRMAA tier. Because both the bracket and the IRMAA threshold are cliffs of a sort, the last dollars converted are the ones to watch. Model the conversion's MAGI impact two years forward for the IRMAA effect, not just the current-year tax.

Not advice: a framework

Conversion and contribution strategy depend on the individual's full tax picture, state taxes, legacy goals, and assumptions about future rates. Use these as factors to evaluate with the client and their tax professional, not as a default recommendation. Farther advisors are not providing tax advice.

Client example: Rosa Alvarez

Suppose Rosa retires and files single. Between her pension and modest IRA withdrawals her MAGI lands around $85,000, comfortably below the $109,000 single-filer IRMAA threshold. In a low-income year before RMDs begin she considers a Roth conversion. A $30,000 conversion pushes MAGI to $115,000, $6,000 over the line, and because IRMAA is a cliff that triggers the full first-tier Part B and Part D surcharge two years later, for the whole year, not a prorated amount. Sizing the conversion at $20,000 keeps her under $109,000 and avoids the surcharge entirely. The last few thousand dollars converted are the ones to watch.

Section 15

Protections & rules to know

PBGC insurance

The pension is federally insured by the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation. If the plan terminated underfunded, PBGC covers normal/early retirement and certain survivor and disability benefits, up to legal limits. Lump sums forfeit this guarantee.

QDRO (divorce)

A Qualified Domestic Relations Order can assign part of the pension to a former spouse. Benefits otherwise can't be assigned, pledged, or garnished by creditors.

Disability pension

Generally available with 15+ years of service if totally disabled and not yet Mod-75 eligible. Paid as a single life annuity, unreduced for early commencement; convertible to a regular pension (with lump-sum option) later.

Survivor benefits

Death before commencement can provide a surviving spouse/partner a benefit (often a 50% J&S equivalent, or a lump sum equal to the cash balance for lump-sum-eligible employees). Beneficiary designations live on Fidelity's system.

Funding-level limits

Federal law can restrict lump sums, plant-shutdown benefits, and benefit increases if plan funding drops below thresholds. The plan reports being funded above those levels; clients get notice if limits ever apply.

No double crediting

Service or pay can't count twice across plan programs; an offset prevents double counting for clients who moved between AT&T entities or programs.

Client example: Rosa Alvarez

If Rosa died before commencing, her husband could receive a survivor benefit, for the Pre-99 portion generally a 50% J&S-equivalent annuity, and for the Cash Balance portion an annuity or a lump sum of the account. Her Fidelity beneficiary designations govern who receives what, so confirming they're current (especially after any marriage, divorce, or death in the family) is a five-minute task that prevents a costly mistake. A QDRO could also assign part of her pension to a former spouse; absent one, the benefit can't be assigned, pledged, or garnished by creditors.

Section 16

Glossary

Cash Balance Account
The hypothetical, account-style portion of the pension. In the Southeast Program it covers service from 1999 on and grows via Basic Benefit Credits and Interest Credits. In Bargained Cash Balance Program #2 it is the entire benefit and also receives Supplemental Pay Credits.
Basic Benefit Credit
The pay- or band-based credit added to a Cash Balance Account. The basis is program-specific: Bargained Cash Balance #2 = Pension Compensation × an Age Credit Factor (monthly); Southeast = 60 × the Pension Band Amount (annual); Mobility = a flat 5% of Pension Compensation (monthly).
Age Credit Factor
The age-based percentage applied to Pension Compensation in Bargained Cash Balance Program #2: 1.77% under 30, 2.27% at 30–36, 2.78% at 37–43, 3.28% at 44–49, 4.04% at 50+. An alternate 1.75%–4.00% ladder applies to the Mobility contracts, IBEW Local 1547, and to earlier service under several contracts. Not used by Southeast, Mobility, East, Legacy Bargained, or Nonbargained.
Interest Credit
Monthly credit compounding to roughly 4.5%/year on the prior balance (or a Treasury-linked rate in some variants).
Supplemental Pay Credit
Extra 2% credit on pay above the Social Security Wage Base.
Pre-99 Pension Band Benefit
The older annuity formula for pre-1999 service: Basic Monthly + Supplemental Monthly Pension Benefit.
Pension Band Amount
A dollar figure tied to job title used in the Pre-99 Basic Monthly Pension Benefit.
Pension Calculation Service
Continuous service used in the Pre-99 formula (measured as of 12/31/1998 for the Basic Monthly piece).
Age Incentive Factor
A multiplier (1.06 at age 65) applied in the Pre-99 Basic Monthly Pension Benefit.
Modified Rule of 75 (Mod 75)
Age/service combinations that grant unreduced or subsidized early retirement on the Pre-99 benefit (and can unlock retiree benefits).
Normal Retirement Age
Later of age 65 or the required years of plan participation.
Benefit Commencement Date
The date pension payments begin.
Vested Interest
The point the benefit is the employee's to keep (3 yrs cash balance / 5 yrs older formula).
Joint & Survivor (J&S)
An annuity that continues a percentage (50/75/100%) to a survivor after the retiree's death.
PBGC
Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation, federal insurer of the pension.
QDRO
Qualified Domestic Relations Order, court order splitting the benefit in divorce.
SPD
Summary Plan Description, the controlling plain-language plan document.
402(g) limit
The annual cap on an employee's own elective deferrals (pre-tax + Roth). $24,500 in 2026.
415(c) limit
The annual cap on all additions to the plan combined, deferrals, employer match, and after-tax contributions. $72,000 in 2026.
After-tax (non-Roth) contributions
A third 401(k) bucket above the deferral limit; only the growth is taxable, which is why it's converted to Roth promptly.
Mega-backdoor Roth
Making after-tax contributions and converting them to Roth (in-plan or to a Roth IRA), turning 415(c) headroom into tax-free Roth money.
Catch-up contribution
Extra deferrals allowed at age 50+ (with an enhanced amount at ages 60–63). Under SECURE 2.0 it must be made as Roth if the client's prior-year FICA wages exceeded $150,000, a rule the ARSP applies now. In the BSSP, which has no Roth, that makes the catch-up limit $0 for those clients.
True-up
An optional plan feature that retroactively pays any match missed because deferrals stopped early in the year. Not all plans offer it.
IRMAA
Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount, an income-based surcharge on Medicare Part B and D premiums, set by MAGI from two years prior.
MAGI
Modified adjusted gross income, the income measure that drives IRMAA tiers; fed by pension income, 401(k) withdrawals, and Roth conversions.
Roth conversion
Moving pre-tax dollars to Roth by recognizing income now in exchange for tax-free growth and lower future MAGI.
Standard Plan Options
The savings plan's core investment lineup, the funds offered directly by the employer, as distinct from the brokerage window.
BrokerageLink / BrokerageLink Roth
Fidelity's self-directed brokerage window inside the plan. From Sept 21, 2026 it exists as two accounts, BrokerageLink for non-Roth balances and BrokerageLink Roth for Roth balances.
Blackout period
More than three consecutive business days in which the right to direct or diversify plan balances, or to take loans or distributions, is suspended or restricted. The BrokerageLink blackout runs Sept 14 to Sept 22, 2026.
SMM
Summary of Material Modifications, the notice that amends an SPD between full reissues. The Roth investment enhancements arrive as an SMM to the July 2026 SPD.
